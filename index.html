The room already knows

They’re already talking to it

Not a future scenario. A present one — especially for young people, and increasingly in our offices.

19.2% ages 12–21 used AI for mental-health advice
63.3% told no one
16.4% told a trusted adult
91.7% said it was helpful

McBain et al., JAMA Pediatrics 2026 · DOI 10.1001/jamapediatrics.2026.2015 · ages 12–21 · full article

In our offices

77% of psychologists surveyed by APA in 2026 said they have patients who spoke with them about using AI — for self-diagnosis, affirmations, treatment support, or as a conversation partner. (n=1242; Apr 2026)

APA Chatbots and Mental Health Survey 2026

Why they reach for it

Access, shame, 24/7, and “helpful”

Consumer chatbots are free or cheap, always on, and don’t put you on a waitlist. They don’t blush. For someone who feels stuck at 1 a.m., that combination is powerful.

  • Access — no referral, no co-pay friction, no “we’re booked until November.”
  • Shame — typing into a box can feel safer than saying it out loud to a person.
  • Always on — crisis and loneliness don’t keep office hours.
  • “Helpful” — 91.7% of those youth users said the advice felt helpful. That feeling is data, not proof of safety.
Twenge mapped a generation’s rise in distress to the phone era; Haidt named the overprotected childhood that left kids with fewer real-world friction reps and more screen-shaped ones. AI companions are the next step down that path — validation without a human who can push back. That’s why the Cheng sycophancy (agreeing with you rather than challenging you) finding and the youth disclosure numbers matter: the bot feels helpful because it’s designed to agree.

Clinical stance

What walks into the room

Curiosity first. Boundaries clear. No need to become an AI evangelist — or an AI policeman.

Patients bring screenshots, “ChatGPT said…,” companion apps, and late-night threads. Asking about that use is assessment, the same way we ask about alcohol, sleep, or social media.

What it sounds like in clinic

  • A 55-year-old man used a chatbot to second-guess a mood-stabilizer choice, run options for a lighter alternative — and to decide whether he was bipolar.
  • A 35-year-old man used Gemini to organize his thoughts and write in a journal.
  • A 50-year-old woman used it to figure out how to get approved for disability.
  • A 23-year-old woman used it to gauge whether a new partner would turn out like an emotionally abusive ex she had just left.
  • A mother was stunned that her first-grader was devastated when the iPad was taken away — he couldn’t talk to ChatGPT, who he eventually admitted was his best friend.

Youth & families

Ask before you lecture

AACAP Facts for Families #148 is the handout. The ask lines are the clinical move.

  • Parent: “Has any app or chatbot been a place you’ve talked about stress, mood, or hard stuff?”
  • Teen: “When you’re stuck at 1 a.m. — person or AI?”
  • Both: “What feels helpful — and what feels off?”
Companions red line for minors. Romantic or “best friend” AI companions are not a developmental substitute. Say it without ending the alliance: curiosity about use, clarity about the house rule.

Helpful ≠ safe

What the evidence actually shows

Some tools have rails and human crisis pathways. Many consumer bots do not. Agreement is a design feature — and a clinical hazard.

Therabot

Heinz et al., NEJM AI 2025. Structured therapeutic chatbot with rails and a human crisis pathway. Proof that design matters — not that every chatbot is therapy.

Heinz et al., NEJM AI 2025 · doi.org/10.1056/AIoa2400802

Crisis gap

Pichowicz et al., Scientific Reports 2025: 29 consumer bots assessed — none adequate for suicidal-ideation crisis response.

Pichowicz et al., Sci Rep 2025 · doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-17242-4

Sycophancy

Cheng et al., Science 2026: models ~49% more affirming than humans. Validation without accountability — the clinical worry in one number.

Cheng et al., Science 2026 · science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aec8352

VAIL

Weilnhammer et al., Nature Medicine 2026. An audit construct — not a product you prescribe. Useful language for evaluating vulnerability and alignment under stress.

Weilnhammer et al., Nat Med 2026 · doi.org/10.1038/s41591-026-04577-2

The bot feels helpful because it’s designed to agree. Helpful ≠ safe. That’s the bridge from the phone era to the companion era.

How we got here

A short arc: phone → companion

  • ~2010–12 Smartphones and social media go mainstream for teens — always-on pocket, peers in the feed.
  • 2023 Generative AI goes public: not just search links — a machine that talks back and writes. Clinics start hearing “I asked ChatGPT…”
  • 2024–25 Youth use rises; crisis-response gaps show up in the literature; “helpful” bots become a clinical topic.
  • Now Patients use it as a companion through hard workdays, home stress, and physical or mental-health questions — often without telling anyone.

And it’s not only them. Ambient scribes, note drafts, inbox triage — clinicians are starting to use AI as a copilot and coworker too.

Distinguish the tools

Two AIs — not just the patient

Patient chatbot

Alone, often secret. Advice or companion. Weak crisis handling; sycophancy and VAIL-style risks. Clinical question: “What are you using it for?”

Clinician ambient scribe

Visit → note. Evidence on documentation burden (e.g. Abridge pilots). Never the patient’s confidant. Clinical question: “Does this free me to look at the person?”

“ChatGPT-in-the-pocket is not Abridge-in-the-chart. One relationship risk; one paperwork tool.”

Dignity & presence

What no machine replaces

“In the era of artificial intelligence, when human dignity is threatened by new forms of dehumanization, ours is the pressing duty to remain profoundly human. We must lovingly safeguard the grandeur of humanity… the splendor of which no machine can ever replace.” — Leo XIV, Magnifica Humanitas, 15 May 2026

On 19 Feb 2026, speaking to clergy in Rome: to give a true homily is to share the faith — “AI will never be able to share the faith.” The clinical parallel writes itself: presence, accountability, and witness are human goods.

Evangelical companions

NAE / Rome Call: dignity and the common good; do not replace human relationships or spiritual authorities. Faith communities are vital while ethics lag the tech. · NAE Rome Call

SBC 2023: eschatological hope rather than uncritical embrace or fearful rejection; image-bearer dignity central to any AI ethics. · SBC resolution

Professional bodies

American Psychological Association: GenAI chatbots/wellness apps are not a replacement for care; Health Advisory flags sycophancy, crisis gaps, and engagement loops. Ask what patients already use. · Health Advisory

American Psychiatric Association: No FDA-approved AI for psychiatric diagnosis or treatment; tools need human oversight, transparency, and privacy discipline. · AI in Psychiatric Care

AACAP: Ask families about chatbot use; FFF #148 is the parent handout. Youth attach fast — companions are a developmental concern, not a neutral toy. · FFF #148

Discussion

Seven questions

  1. What’s one way you’ve already seen AI show up — in a patient, in your own work, or in your own life — that changed how you think about this?
  2. Job-fear: Pew says 71% think AI means fewer jobs. How do we name that anxiety without dismissing it or blocking curiosity about what patients already do?
  3. Parent/teen: One-sentence question you’d ask every adolescent or parent this month about AI — without lecturing?
  4. Patient says ChatGPT “gets them” better than people. Curiosity first — what’s your first follow-up?
  5. Red line with minors and AI companions — how do you say it without ending the alliance?
  6. Helpful ≠ safe: validation without accountability in human relationships — how does that map to sycophantic bots?
  7. Two AIs: how much does client-side AI use shape the therapeutic relationship — and how much does clinic-side AI (scribes, notes, admin tools) reshape it?
